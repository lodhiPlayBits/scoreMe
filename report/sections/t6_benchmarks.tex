\section{Task 6: Empirical Analysis and Benchmarking}

\textbf{Setup.} Python 3.12 on one core of an ARM64 Linux VM. Every row runs
through \texttt{run.solve}, the same code path as \texttt{python run.py
...}. \texttt{tools/bench.py} regenerates every number and figure in this
section; its raw output is in \texttt{report/data/}. Runtime is the median
of 3 runs. ``OPT'' is the exact optimum of $P$ from the branch-and-bound
solver (\texttt{solver/bruteforce.py}). That solver is cross-checked against
plain $K^n$ enumeration in \texttt{tests/test\_validate.py} and
\texttt{tests/test\_penalty.py}.

\textbf{Feasibility.} Every \texttt{feasible=true} output passes the
independent validator (\texttt{solver/validate.py}). The exact solver
agrees with every verdict it can check: OPT exists for $n\le12$, and
branch-and-bound confirms nothing beyond what SSD-R reports.

\subsection{Anomalies}
\paragraph{A1 --- six of nine benchmarks are infeasible.}
This is the main finding, and it is a property of the provided generator,
not a failure of SSD-R. Each \texttt{violation\_reason} is a certificate
that a reader can check by hand:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{seed 10} ($n{=}50$): the triangle $T3$--$T8$--$T23$ has every
  window equal to $[3,4]$. Three mutually conflicting tasks cannot share
  2 slots.
\item \textbf{seed 11} ($n{=}100$): the 4-clique $\{T13, T14, T25, T59\}$ is
  confined to the 3 slots $[7,9]$.
\item \textbf{seed 12} ($n{=}150$) and \textbf{seed 20} ($n{=}200, K{=}15$):
  network demand is $82.8 > 72$ and $94.6 > 90$ Gbps over all slots. This is
  \emph{structural}. The generator draws $r_k\sim U(1,\lfloor \text{cap}_k /
  (\lfloor n/K\rfloor+1)\rfloor)$. When that floor is 0
  ($\lfloor 6/13\rfloor = \lfloor 6/14\rfloor = 0$), Python's
  \texttt{uniform(1,0)} returns values in $[0,1]$ with mean $0.5$. Expected
  Net demand is then $0.5n$, against $6K$ of capacity: $75>72$ and
  $100>90$. We confirmed that these two $(n,K)$ pairs are infeasible at
  every density in $\{0.02, 0.05, 0.1\}$ for seeds 0--4.
\item \textbf{seed 21} ($n{=}200, K{=}5$): the triangle $T10$--$T67$--$T73$
  lies inside $[0,1]$. The same instance also fails capacity in two
  dimensions, GPU $103.8>40$ and Net $98.2>30$; Stage 1 reports the first
  certificate it finds.
\item \textbf{seed 22} ($n{=}200, K{=}20$): the 5-cycle
  $T102$--$T170$--$T57$--$T198$--$T63$ lies inside $[18,19]$. An odd cycle
  needs 3 colours, and only 2 slots are available. A single greedy clique
  pass \emph{missed} this case: the only triangle in it,
  $\{T63, T102, T198\}$, was not found because the greedy started from the
  highest-degree vertex. This led us to make the width-2 check an exact
  bipartiteness test (L4).
\end{itemize}
\emph{Why so many?} 31\% of all generator windows have width 2, and 50\%
have width $\le 3$. If $t$ tasks are confined to a 2-slot window, they
contain a triangle with probability roughly $1-(1-p^3)^{\binom{t}{3}}$
(ignoring dependence between triangles). At $p=0.25$ this is 0.27 for
$t=6$ and 0.59 for $t=8$. In seed 10, windows of exactly width 2 hold 2--7
tasks each (7 in $[6,7]$). There are $K-1$ such windows, and the odd-cycle
test also catches cycles longer than 3, so the probability that
\emph{some} window fails is high. The certificates come from
Stage~1 alone, in 2.6--256~ms. Without them, a heuristic would either
search the 6 instances until it timed out or report ``not found'' with no
reason.

\paragraph{A2 --- seed 1 ratio 1.141.}
SSD-R puts $T3$ ($w{=}2.80$) in slot 0, and $T5$ ($w{=}5.94$) and $T6$
($w{=}4.64$) in slot 1. The optimum swaps them: $T3\to1$, $T5,T6\to0$.
$T3$ conflicts with \emph{both} $T5$ and $T6$, so every single move and
every pairwise swap is infeasible. Swapping $T3$ with $T5$ puts $T3$ next
to $T6$. Reaching the optimum needs a simultaneous 3-task exchange, which
is a Kempe-chain interchange of slots 0 and 1 on the component
$\{T3,T5,T6\}$. This is the same structure as our tight example (\S4) on a
real benchmark: L8 commits the conflict hub $T3$ (degree 4, the highest)
first, and L19--L20 cannot undo it.

\paragraph{A3 --- runtime is not monotone in $n$.}
The infeasible rows are fast, 2.6~ms at $n{=}200$ for seed 21, because
Stage~1 returns as soon as the first certificate is found. Intervals are
scanned from $[0,0]$ upward, so a certificate in a low window is found
early. Seed 22 takes 256~ms because its certificate is in the last window,
$[18,19]$, so all $O(K^2)$ intervals, each with a clique search, run first.

\subsection{Scaling on feasible instances}
None of the graded instances above $n{=}12$ is feasible, so they cannot
show how the constructive and improvement stages scale. We therefore ran
20 supplementary instances: $(n,K)\in\{(25,6),(50,8),(100,10),(200,20)\}$,
density 0.05, seeds 0--4. 19 are feasible and solved. One, $(100,10)$ seed 2,
is proven infeasible by the \emph{sub-interval} demand check: tasks confined
to $[1,9]$ need 54.95 Gbps against 54.0. The global check over $[0,9]$
passes, which is why L3 scans all intervals.

\textbf{Penalty} grows roughly as $n^2/K$. $P_{\text{base}}$ sums $w_i(\sigma_i+1)$,
and with $K$ growing alongside $n$ the average slot index also grows.
\textbf{Runtime} on feasible instances fits $t\propto n^{2.33}$ on a
log-log scale. This is below the worst-case $O(n^3Kd)$ of \S3, because
restarts are rare (none were needed on these 19 instances), so the
practical cost is one constructive pass ($O(n^2Kd)$) plus local search
($O(n^2d)$ per pass).

\subsection{Approximation quality vs the exact optimum}
To go beyond three small data points, we ran SSD-R and the exact solver on
1200 random small instances: $(n,K,\text{dens})\in\{(6,3,.3),(8,3,.3),(8,4,.4),
(10,4,.4),(10,3,.2),(12,4,.5)\}$, seeds 0--199
(\texttt{report/data/small\_study.csv}).

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{l rr}
\toprule
& exact: feasible & exact: infeasible\\
\midrule
SSD-R feasible & 823 & 0\\
SSD-R proven infeasible & 0 & 316\\
SSD-R not found & 1 & 60\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Soundness:} no false certificates (0 in the top-right cell)
  and no invalid outputs (0 in the middle-left cell).
\item \textbf{Completeness of Stage 1:} 316 of 376 infeasible instances
  (84\%) are certified. The other 60 need a clique larger than the greedy
  finds, or a colouring argument that is not a clique. They are honestly
  reported as \texttt{NOT\_FOUND}.
\item \textbf{Missed feasible:} 1 in 824 (0.12\%), seed 171 of
  $(12,4,0.5)$. Its exact solution is essentially a unique 4-colouring of a
  dense graph, with six tasks restricted to 2--3 slot windows. The restart
  promotions cycle through six tasks
  ($T4\to T3\to T11\to T8\to T6\to T10\to T4\dots$), and each promoted task
  displaces the next. During development this count was 58, and two fixes
  (\S3) reduced it. Restarts (R1--R3) brought it to 2. Forward checking
  (L10) brought it to 1; it fixed the restart cycle on $(100,10,0.05)$
  seed 1, where four tasks with nested windows evicted each other in turn.
\item \textbf{Ratio} over the 823 jointly feasible instances: mean 1.054,
  median 1.000, 95th percentile 1.242, maximum 1.644. 59.1\% are solved
  exactly.
\end{itemize}
