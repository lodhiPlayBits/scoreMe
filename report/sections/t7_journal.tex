\section{Task 7: Design Journal}

\subsection{The hardest design decision: what Stage 1 is allowed to claim}
The toughest call was in Stage 1 and it wasn't about speed. It was on
\emph{what my algorithm is allowed to say}. My first version had a single
greedy clique check (largest degree first) per interval. I ran it on
benchmark seed 22 ($n{=}200$, $K{=}20$). SSD-R placed 6 tasks and returned
\texttt{NOT\_FOUND} on $T63$.

I had to make a decision then. One possibility was to increase the search
effort, by performing more restarts or a deeper repair, and treat the
instance as hard. The other was to treat it as possibly impossible and
strengthen the \emph{proof}.

Examining the tasks confined to window $[18,19]$ settled it. $T63$, $T102$
and $T198$ form a triangle, and my greedy clique, starting from the highest
degree vertex, had walked by it. No search could ever have been successful.
The trade-off was between a general but incomplete certificate (cliques for
every width) and an exact but narrow one. I picked both. Colorability of
width-2 intervals is precisely bipartiteness, which can be decided by BFS
in linear time. So I made that case exact (odd-cycle certificate) and kept
the clique heuristic only for width $\ge3$. The alternative I discarded was
a small exact coloring search over each interval. It is more complete, but
it is exponential in the worst case, and it would blur the clean line I
wanted: Stage 1 is polynomial and never wrong, while Stages 2--4 are
polynomial and may give up. The deciding factor was the data: 31\% of
generator windows are width 2, so the exact case covers the most common
situation. After the change, all six large benchmarks have a certificate
that a reviewer can check manually.

\subsection{Where SSD-R failed empirically, and the next week}
\textbf{Failure 1 --- benchmark $(8,3,0.3)$, seed 1, ratio 1.141.} SSD-R
assigns the conflict hub $T3$ (degree 4, weight 2.8) to slot 0, and moves
$T5$ (5.94) and $T6$ (4.64) to slot 1. The optimum swaps them, but $T3$
conflicts with both of them, so no single move or pair swap is possible.
Local search is stuck in a 3-task trap. My own tight example (\S4) has the
same structure; I had built it on paper before seeing it in a benchmark.

\textbf{Failure 2 --- random instance $(12,4,0.5)$, seed 171:
\texttt{NOT\_FOUND} on a feasible instance.} The squeaky-wheel restarts
cycle: $T4\to T3\to T11\to T8\to T6\to T10\to T4$. Each promoted task takes
its cheapest slot, and that evicts the next one. Earlier in development,
the same failure mode cost 58 of 824 feasible instances. Restarts brought
that to 2, and forward checking at L10 brought it to 1. This one survives
because its solution is essentially a unique 4-colouring.

\textbf{With one more week} I would add \emph{Kempe-chain moves} to
Stage 4: swap two slots on a whole connected component of the two-slot
conflict subgraph. That move fixes Failure 1 directly. Its catch is that
it would also escape my current tight example, so I would need to find a
new one and re-prove the tightness level. For Failure 2, I would add an
exact backtracking fallback restricted to the tasks involved in a restart
cycle. It is exponential only in that small set, and it returns to the
greedy afterwards.

\subsection{Where this appears at ScoreMe}
The closest match is the \textbf{OCR GPU cluster fed by a Kafka consumer
group}. Bank-statement OCR jobs arrive on a Kafka topic, and each partition
is consumed by one consumer per execution cycle. Mapped onto our problem:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Slots} are the 30-second scheduling cycles of the GPU pool.
\item \emph{Resources} are GPU units (OCR models pinned to cards), CPU
  cores for pre-processing, RAM for page images, and network bandwidth for
  pulling statements from object storage.
\item \emph{Conflicts} are two jobs that write to the same Kafka partition,
  where ordering must hold, or that share a GPU memory bus.
\item \emph{SLA windows} come from the lender's turnaround commitment. A
  statement uploaded at $T{=}0$ for a Tier-1 bank must be parsed within $k$
  cycles.
\end{itemize}
SSD-R would run once per planning horizon, for example every 10 cycles,
over the queued jobs. Stage~1 is the most valuable part operationally. If
the queued work \emph{cannot} meet its SLAs, it reports exactly which jobs
collide and where: ``these 5 statements form an odd conflict cycle inside a
2-cycle window'', or ``Net demand over cycles 1--9 exceeds the link''.
This can be acted on. The platform team can add a new GPU node, rebalance
the topic or even warn the lender ahead of the SLA breaching. The $R$ term
of $P$ ensures that jobs don't get too close to their SLA limit. This means
that one OCR retry won't cause any SLA issues. The $B$ term ensures that we
don't overload the GPUs in one cycle and leave the next cycle waiting.

\subsection{What surprised me}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The benchmarks are generally impossible to solve.} 6 out of
  9 benchmarks. 2 of these are impossible to solve due to something outside
  of the scheduling problem itself. The generator uses
  \texttt{uniform(1, cap//(n//K+1))}, so for $n{=}150$ and $K{=}12$ the
  maximum value is $6//13=0$. Python then automatically samples from the
  range $[0,1]$. The Net demand exceeds capacity at all conflict densities.
  I was expecting the stress tests to be stressful for the heuristic.
  However, they were stressful for the infeasibility detection part of the
  algorithm.
\item \textbf{Overall, proving that an instance was impossible to solve,
  quickly, was more useful than optimising.} Most of the work was done in
  Stage 1 and in understanding failure modes.
\item \textbf{The approximation ratio does not reward any of the smart
  parts.} It is purely based on feasibility: all tasks fit within their
  time window. Nothing is contributed by the greedy or local search
  algorithms. However, I only realised this after seeing a tight example
  which showed that SSD-R hits the bound exactly, and then seeing this trap
  occur in an actual benchmark.
\item \textbf{In my thinking process} the first thought was to add more
  search to overcome a failure. However, all actual improvements came from
  first checking if the instance was even solvable, and comparing against
  the exact solver.
\end{itemize}
