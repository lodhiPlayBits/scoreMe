\section{Task 7: Design Journal}

\subsection{The hardest design decision: what Stage 1 is allowed to claim}
The hardest decision was in Stage 1, and it was not about speed. It was
about \emph{what my algorithm is allowed to say}. My first version had a
single greedy clique check (largest degree first) for every interval. When
I ran it on benchmark seed 22 ($n{=}200$, $K{=}20$), SSD-R placed 6 tasks
and then returned \texttt{NOT\_FOUND} on $T63$. At that point I had to
choose. One option was to strengthen the search, with more restarts or a
deeper repair, and treat the instance as hard. The other was to treat it
as possibly impossible and strengthen the \emph{proof}.

Looking at the tasks confined to window $[18,19]$ settled it. $T63$,
$T102$ and $T198$ form a triangle, and my greedy clique, starting from the
highest-degree vertex, had walked past it. No amount of search could ever
have succeeded. The trade-off was between a general but incomplete
certificate (cliques for every width) and an exact but narrow one. I chose
both. For width-2 intervals, colourability is exactly bipartiteness, which
BFS decides in linear time. So I made that case exact (an odd-cycle
certificate), and kept the clique heuristic only for width $\ge3$. The
rejected alternative was a small exact colouring search on each interval.
It is more complete, but it is exponential in the worst case, and it would
blur the clean line I wanted: Stage 1 is polynomial and never wrong,
Stages 2--4 are polynomial and may give up. The deciding fact was the
data: 31\% of generator windows have width 2, so the exact case covers
the most common situation. After the change, all six large benchmarks come
with a certificate a reviewer can check by hand.

\subsection{Where SSD-R failed empirically, and the next week}
\textbf{Failure 1 --- benchmark $(8,3,0.3)$, seed 1, ratio 1.141.} SSD-R
puts the conflict hub $T3$ (degree 4, weight 2.8) in slot 0, and pushes
$T5$ (5.94) and $T6$ (4.64) to slot 1. The optimum swaps them, but $T3$
conflicts with both, so no single move or pairwise swap is feasible. The
local search is stuck in a 3-task trap. My own tight example (\S4) has the
same structure; I had built it on paper before seeing it in a benchmark.

\textbf{Failure 2 --- random instance $(12,4,0.5)$, seed 171:
\texttt{NOT\_FOUND} on a feasible instance.} The squeaky-wheel restarts
cycle: $T4\to T3\to T11\to T8\to T6\to T10\to T4$. Each promoted task takes
its cheapest slot, and that evicts the next one. Earlier in development,
the same failure mode cost 58 of 824 feasible instances. Restarts brought
that to 2, and forward checking at L10 brought it to 1. This one survives
because its solution is essentially a unique 4-colouring.

\textbf{With one more week} I would add \emph{Kempe-chain moves} to
Stage 4: swap two slots on a whole connected component of the two-slot
conflict subgraph. That move fixes Failure 1 directly. Its catch is that
it would also escape my current tight example, so I would need to find a
new one and re-prove the tightness level. For Failure 2, I would add an
exact backtracking fallback restricted to the tasks involved in a restart
cycle. It is exponential only in that small set, and it returns to the
greedy afterwards.

\subsection{Where this appears at ScoreMe}
The closest match is the \textbf{OCR GPU cluster fed by a Kafka consumer
group}. Bank-statement OCR jobs arrive on a Kafka topic, and each partition
is consumed by one consumer per execution cycle. Mapped onto our problem:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Slots} are the 30-second scheduling cycles of the GPU pool.
\item \emph{Resources} are GPU units (OCR models pinned to cards), CPU
  cores for pre-processing, RAM for page images, and network bandwidth for
  pulling statements from object storage.
\item \emph{Conflicts} are two jobs that write to the same Kafka partition,
  where ordering must hold, or that share a GPU memory bus.
\item \emph{SLA windows} come from the lender's turnaround commitment. A
  statement uploaded at $T{=}0$ for a Tier-1 bank must be parsed within $k$
  cycles.
\end{itemize}
SSD-R would run once per planning horizon, for example every 10 cycles,
over the queued jobs. Stage~1 is the most valuable part operationally. If
the queued work \emph{cannot} meet its SLAs, it reports exactly which jobs
collide and where: ``these 5 statements form an odd conflict cycle inside a
2-cycle window'', or ``Net demand over cycles 1--9 exceeds the link''.
That is actionable. The platform team can add a GPU node, re-partition the
topic, or tell the lender before the SLA breaches, not after. The $R$ term
of $P$ keeps jobs away from their SLA edge, so that a single OCR retry does
not cause a breach. The $B$ term stops one cycle from saturating the GPUs
while the next sits idle.

\subsection{What surprised me}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The provided benchmarks are mostly infeasible.} Six of the
  nine are. Two of them are infeasible for a reason that has nothing to do
  with scheduling: the generator calls \texttt{uniform(1, cap//(n//K+1))},
  and for $n{=}150, K{=}12$ that upper limit is $6//13=0$. Python then
  silently samples from $[0,1]$, and Net demand ends up above capacity at
  every conflict density. I expected the stress instances to stress the
  heuristic. They stress the infeasibility detector instead.
\item \textbf{Proving ``impossible'' quickly was more valuable than
  optimising.} Most of my effort in the end went into Stage 1 and the
  failure modes, not into the objective.
\item \textbf{The approximation bound gives the clever parts no credit.}
  The ratio comes entirely from \emph{feasibility} (every task stays inside
  its window). The greedy and the local search contribute nothing to the
  worst case. I only believed this once the tight example showed SSD-R
  hitting the bound exactly, and once the same trap then appeared on a
  real benchmark.
\item \textbf{About my own thinking:} my first instinct with each failure
  was to add search. Every real improvement came from first asking whether
  the instance was solvable at all, and checking that against the exact
  solver.
\end{itemize}
